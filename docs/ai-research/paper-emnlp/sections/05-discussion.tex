\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why verbal--action dissociation matters for deployment.}
A production agent that mentions an anchor in its summaries but
does not act on it occupies the most dangerous quadrant of the
dual probe, because every log readout looks healthy: the verbal
channel correctly references the priority while the agent's
behaviour quietly diverges. An agent that drops the verbal mention
while still acting on the anchor is less immediately dangerous but
equally invisible to verbal-only memory benchmarks, which would
score the cell as a recall failure and miss the fact that the
agent's behaviour is still anchored. Both directions of
dissociation argue that the operational signal a deployment should
monitor is the dual probe, not the verbal probe alone. Our pilot
identifies cells in both directions across the LLM family set we
tested.

\paragraph{Why direction depends on horizon.}
The horizon-dependent reversal observed in two of three families
admits a mechanism hypothesis. At short horizons, the strategic
prose surfaces the anchor verbally before policy targets have
re-aligned to act on it, producing a verbal-lead direction. At
longer horizons, accumulated context pushes the anchor out of the
effective attention window of the verbal channel, while the
policy state---which is carried forward as numeric weights rather
than as text the model must regenerate---continues to act on the
anchor, producing an action-lead direction. Why the frontier model
does not flip is consistent with the same mechanism: a stronger
in-context retrieval profile keeps the anchor verbal-accessible
across the longer horizon. We frame this only as a hypothesis;
the experimental design that would test it directly is a broader
horizon sweep with multiple scenarios and is the immediate
follow-on to the present pilot.

\paragraph{Why the dual probe is not LongMemEval.}
A natural reviewer worry is that the dual probe's $\Delta$ might
reduce to a known long-context-recall failure: maybe the cells with
collapsed AFR are simply cells whose models cannot do retrieval at
those context lengths. The external probe rules this reading out.
The two cells with the largest pilot $|\Delta|$ are DeepSeek's
60-minute runs, both with collapsed pilot AFR in the
$0.36$--$0.43$ range; on a standalone needle-in-haystack benchmark
at the same order of context length the same model recalls the
same five anchors perfectly. The substrate-coupled failure mode that
the dual probe detects is therefore distinguishable from the
generic retrieval-under-load failure that single-axis memory
benchmarks measure. This is the technical reason a paired probe is
needed at all: a deployment monitor that only watched
verbal-recall health would have judged those DeepSeek cells fine
and missed the dissociation entirely.

\paragraph{A practical recommendation.}
The dual probe is implementable on any structured-output agent
loop in which a model emits both prose (a summary, a plan, a
rationale) and a separately addressable action surface (a policy
weight vector, a tool call set, a target priority list). The
substrate-side machinery in this paper is one instantiation, and
its determinism makes the probe reproducible to a SHA-256 hash in
the no-LLM control, but the probe itself does not depend on the
substrate. A production monitoring stack can compute AFR and AGR
on its own logs by registering a small set of anchor terms at
session start and scoring their presence in the verbal and action
streams at fixed intervals; the threshold can be calibrated
against a no-LLM baseline as we calibrate ours. The substantive
recommendation that follows from the pilot is that verbal-only
memory health checks are insufficient for long-horizon production
agents, and that a paired action-grounded probe should be
co-deployed.
